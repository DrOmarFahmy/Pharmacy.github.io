Chapter 1: Rheology

What is Rheology?

Rheology is the branch of physical pharmacy that studies the flow of liquids and deformation of semisolids.

The word comes from Greek: Rheo = to flow Logos = science

  • Applies to gases, liquids, and semi-solids
  • Solids are completely resistant to flow and deformation
🔑 Trick: Think of honey vs water on a glass — honey flows slowly = high viscosity. Water flows fast = low viscosity.

Viscosity & Fluidity

  • Viscosity (η) = resistance of a fluid to flow. Unit: Poise or centipoise (cp)
  • Fluidity (φ) = ability to flow = reciprocal of viscosity
φ = 1/η     η = F/G

1 Poise = 100 centipoise (cp). The shearing force needed to produce velocity of 1 cm/sec between two layers each 1 cm² in area and 1 cm apart.

🔑 Trick: Higher viscosity = harder to flow. Lower fluidity = thicker liquid.

Applications of Rheology

General
  • Paints, Inks, Road-building materials, Dairy products
Pharmaceutical
  • Solutions, suspensions, emulsions, cosmetics
  • Formulation of pastes, suppositories, tablet coatings
  • Creams, pastes, and lotions — batch-to-batch consistency
Industrial
  • Mixing & flow of materials
  • Packaging into containers
  • Pouring liquids from a bottle
  • Extrusion of ointments/creams from tubes
  • Passage of liquid from ampoule through syringe needle

Newton's Law of Flow

If a block of liquid consists of parallel plates and force F' is applied to surface area A:

  • Shearing Stress (F) = F'/A (dyne/cm²) — force per unit area
  • Shearing Rate (G) = dv/dr (sec⁻¹) — velocity difference between layers
F'/A = η × dv/dr    →    η = F/G
  • Dynamic viscosity (η): resistance to flow = F/G. Unit: poise (dyne·sec·cm⁻²)
  • Kinematic viscosity (s) = η/ρ. Unit: Stoke, centistoke
  • Relative viscosity (ηr) = η/η₀ (ratio to water) — unitless
  • Specific viscosity (ηsp) = ηr − 1 — unitless
🔑 Exam Trick: For Newtonian liquids — single-point measurement is ENOUGH to find viscosity. This is a common exam question!

Viscosity & Temperature (Arrhenius Equation)

Temperature increase → viscosity DECREASES for liquids.

η = A · eEv/RT
  • A = constant depending on molecular weight & molar volume
  • Ev = activation energy required to initiate flow
  • R = gas constant (0.082057 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)
  • T = temperature in Kelvin (K = °C + 273)
🔑 Trick: Higher temperature → molecules have more energy → flow more easily → viscosity drops.

🔑 Trick: Newtonian = straight line through origin = one test is enough. Non-Newtonian = curve or shifted = need multiple points.

1) Plastic Flow (Bingham Flow)

Non-NewtonianShear-Thinning
  • Materials = Bingham Bodies (named after Bingham, the first investigator)
  • Rheogram does NOT start from origin — starts slightly curved, then becomes straight
  • The x-axis intercept of the straight part = Yield Value (f) or Bingham Yield Value
  • Flow only occurs when stress exceeds the yield value
U = (F − f) / G
  • U = plastic viscosity
  • f = yield value
  • Mobility = slope of straight part (like fluidity in Newtonian)

Why yield value? Flocculated suspensions have particles linked by van der Waals forces. You need enough force to break these bonds before flow begins.

🔑 Trick: Higher flocculation → higher yield value. Plastic materials are shear-thinning → more shear = more mobility = lower viscosity. Classic example: flocculated suspension.

2) Pseudo-plastic Flow

Non-NewtonianShear-Thinning
  • Exhibited by polymers in solution: gum tragacanth, sodium alginate, methylcellulose (MC), sodium CMC
  • Rheogram starts from origin (or very near it) — no yield value, no straight line
  • Viscosity CANNOT be expressed by a single value
  • As shear increases → long-chain molecules align in flow direction → viscosity decreases
F = η'·GN    →    log G = N·log F − log η'
  • η' = viscosity coefficient
  • N = exponent: equals 1 for Newtonian, >1 for Pseudo-plastic
🔑 Trick: N=1 → Newtonian. N>1 → Pseudo-plastic. N<1 → Dilatant. Remember: Martin's equation!

3) Dilatant Flow

Non-NewtonianShear-Thickening
  • These systems increase in volume when shear stress is applied ("dilate")
  • Viscosity increases with shear force → opposite of plastic/pseudo-plastic!
  • When stress is removed → system returns to original fluidity (reversible)
  • Rheogram starts from origin, no yield value, no straight line

Mechanism: At rest → particles are closely packed, vehicle fills voids → system can flow.
Under shear → particles expand/dilate → increased void volume → vehicle no longer fills voids → particles no longer lubricated → resistance increases → sets up as firm paste.

N < 1 (in Martin's equation F = η'·GN)

Classic example: Deflocculated suspension with ≥50% solids.

🔑 Exam Question — "Why use high-speed mixers for dilatant materials?" Because dilatant systems thicken under shear. The particles have more time to separate and lubricate at high speed, preventing the paste from setting up solid. Must be handled with care during processing!

Thixotropy (Time-Dependent Behavior)

ReversibleTime-dependent
  • Term first used by Peterfi (1927) — Greek: thixis (stirring) + trepo (changing)
  • Definition: Reversible isothermal transformation of gel → sol → gel
  • Shear stress applied → gel becomes sol. Stress removed → sol returns to gel (takes time)
  • The up-curve and down-curve are NOT superimposed → form a hysteresis loop
  • Wider hysteresis loop = more time & thermal changes needed for recovery

Examples: Suspension, emulsion, microcrystalline cellulose gel

Anti-Thixotropy (Irreversible Thixotropy):

When shear is applied → gel → sol, but when stress removed → sol NEVER returns to gel.
Example: gel from high M.wt. polysaccharides

🔑 Clinical Application: Penicillin G suspension 40–70% w/v:
Dissolve powder in water (gel) → Shake & draw into syringe (sol) → Inject into muscle (returns to gel) → Depot action = slow drug release 💉

Chapter 2: Adsorption

Key Definitions

  • Adsorption: Accumulation of a substance at the surface or interface between two immiscible phases → forms a molecular/atomic film.
  • Adsorbent: The solid on which adsorption occurs (porous material with large specific surface, g/m²)
  • Adsorbate: The substance (gas, vapor, liquid, or solid) that gets adsorbed
  • Desorption: The reverse process — adsorbate leaves the surface
  • Sorption: Combined term for both adsorption + absorption
🔑 Adsorption vs Absorption:
Adsorption = surface attachment (drug at receptor site, membrane surface)
Absorption = entering bulk phase (drug absorbed by blood, tissues, organs)

Why Does Adsorption Happen?

Surface atoms of a solid have bonding deficiency — they are not completely surrounded by other atoms. So they bond whatever is available to lower surface energy.

Two types of forces lead to two types of adsorption:

🔑 Trick: Physisorption = Physical = Weak = Reversible = Many layers.
Chemisorption = Chemical = Strong = Irreversible = One layer.

Factors Affecting Adsorption

  • 1. Surface area of solid: More surface area → more adsorption. Increased by: size reduction (finely divided particles) or porosity (porous material has more surface)
  • 2. Pressure (gas): Amount adsorbed increases with gas pressure
  • 3. Solute concentration: Amount adsorbed (x/m) is directly proportional to concentration
  • 4. Temperature: Physisorption is exothermic → higher temp → less adsorption. Chemisorption increases with temperature (needs activation energy Ea)
  • 5. Solubility of adsorbate (Lundelius' Rule): Higher solubility → less adsorption (adsorbate prefers to stay in solution). Example: methylene blue adsorption from aqueous solution > ethanol solution
  • 6. pH: pH affects solute dissociation. Non-dissociated molecules adsorb better than ions. Amphiphilic compounds adsorb most at their isoelectric point (minimum solubility)
🔑 Lundelius' Rule: The more soluble the adsorbate, the LESS it adsorbs. Think of it this way: if it loves the solvent, why would it stick to the surface?

Adsorption Isotherms

An isotherm = a quantitative relationship between solution concentration of the sorbate and its uptake by adsorbent, measured at constant temperature.

3 Main Types:

Type I — Langmuir

  • Characterized by a horizontal plateau of limited value after rapid initial adsorption
  • For microporous materials — no multilayer adsorption
  • Represents chemisorption
  • Example: water and organic vapors on molecular sieves
  • Langmuir assumptions: only monolayer, uniform surface with equal active sites, adsorption is localized, adsorbate molecules don't interact, heat of adsorption is independent of surface coverage

Type II — Freundlich & BET

  • Describes physical adsorption (physisorption)
  • Monolayer at low pressures → multilayer at higher pressures near saturation
  • Observed in macro-porous solids (pore diameter >50 nm)
  • Example: vapors of polar media on molecular sieves
Freundlich: Y = x/m = KC1/n = KP1/n
Log form: log Y = log K + (1/n) log C
Plot log Y vs log C → slope = 1/n, intercept = log K

BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller)

  • Considers multilayers of gas adsorbed
  • Sigmoidal shape (belongs to Type II)
  • Occurs when gases undergo physical adsorption onto non-porous solid → monolayer first, then multilayer
🔑 Trick: Langmuir = Mono-layer = Chemisorption (L = L). Freundlich & BET = Multi-layer = Physisorption.

Pharmaceutical Applications of Adsorption

  • Decolorizing agent: activated charcoal removes colored impurities from solutions
  • Desiccants and drying agents: adsorb moisture
  • Determination of surface area of powders: using BET isotherm
  • Adsorption chromatography: separation of compounds
  • Drug formulation: drug adsorption on excipients can affect release; activated charcoal in GI tract for poisoning
📊 All Graphs Explained
Graph 1: Newtonian Rheogram (F vs G)

Key points: Straight line passing through the origin. Slope = fluidity (φ = 1/η). The relationship between F and G is directly proportional and constant. One measurement is enough to define the flow. Examples: water, glycerin, oils.

Graph 2: Viscosity vs Shear Rate — Newtonian Systems

For Newtonian liquids, viscosity does NOT change with shear rate — it stays constant (flat horizontal line). This is why single-point measurement is sufficient.

Graph 3: Non-Newtonian Rheograms (All Types on One Plot)

Plastic (Bingham): Does NOT start at origin. Has a yield value (f) on F-axis. Then straight line.
Pseudo-plastic: Starts at/near origin. Concave curve. Higher shear → lower viscosity (shear thinning).
Dilatant: Starts at origin. Convex curve. Higher shear → higher viscosity (shear thickening).

Graph 4: Thixotropy — Hysteresis Loop

The up-curve (increasing shear) and down-curve (decreasing shear) are NOT superimposed → they form a hysteresis loop. The area of the loop represents the energy needed to break down the structure. Wider loop = more time needed for recovery = more thixotropic.

Graph 5: Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm (Log-Log Plot)

Plot log Y vs log C gives a straight line. Slope = 1/n. Y-intercept = log K. This is only valid at low to moderate concentrations/pressures — NOT at high values. Represents physisorption with multilayer.

Graph 6: Langmuir Isotherm (Type I) — Chemisorption

Type I / Langmuir: Rapid initial adsorption → horizontal plateau (saturation). Represents monolayer chemisorption on microporous materials. Once all active sites are filled, no more adsorption occurs. Example: water vapor on molecular sieves.

Graph 7: BET Isotherm (Type II) — Physisorption

Type II / BET: Sigmoidal shape. First a monolayer forms, then multilayers build up near saturation. Represents physisorption on macro-porous solids (pore diameter >50 nm). Used to determine surface area of powders in pharmacy.
